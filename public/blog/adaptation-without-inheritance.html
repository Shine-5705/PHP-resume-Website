What Agents Forget on Purpose

Aug 2026 8 min read
From my paper, Adaptation Without Inheritance
Research Self-Improving Agents Evolution
📄 Read the Research Paper (PDF) → OpenReview →

What your body learns in your lifetime does not get written into the genes your children inherit. Biologists call that separation the Weismann barrier. I wanted to know what happens if you build the same barrier into a population of self-improving coding agents - on purpose, by construction, not as an afterthought.

The assumption I set out to break

The Darwin Gödel Machine lineage replaces the original Gödel machine's proof obligation with empirical validation: a node in the search is scored as an exchangeable set of task outcomes. Written down as an assumption, that's time-homogeneity - the idea that a node's score today would look the same tomorrow, memory and all. I broke that by construction. Each member of my evolving population gets a bounded lifetime, split down the middle by a Weismann barrier. The germline - the agent's code and a handful of tuning numbers - crosses into the child. The soma - everything the agent writes down for itself from its own failures during that one lifetime - is destroyed at the generation boundary.

Separating innate from learned

Inside one lifetime, a failure can climb a small ladder: from a note, to a rule once it recurs and the fix is shown to replay, to a hook, a small program. Re-evaluating the best agents twice - once with memory wiped, once intact - separates what they innately are from what they merely learned during the run. I also built in a preregistered liveness gate that refuses to read a null result through machinery it cannot first show was actually running. That gate matters more than it sounds: it's the difference between "the effect isn't there" and "my harness silently stopped measuring."

The honest result. The smallest effect this design could detect, at this scale, exceeds the kill line I preregistered before running it. This single-seed run cannot distinguish the two arms. I'm saying that plainly rather than reading a signal into noise.

What survived the null result anyway

A null on the headline question doesn't mean the run taught me nothing. Across eight live runs and about 3.4 million tokens:

  • A scored node costs roughly 3× as much with memory as without it, and over half of that spend goes to the inner loop rather than to attempting the task itself.
  • Replaying tasks the agent had already failed - an upper bound on what a live note can do - a 7B-parameter task model applying a correct hand-written rule hits 59.3% against an 11.1% base rate. Its own notes, even after a reflection prompt I had to repair, reach only 25.9%.
  • The promotion ladder's top rung fired 96 times and installed nothing - one deterministic failure replayed over and over, not a capability wall.
  • A preregistered paired check across the whole rule pool scored 5 of 8 with the constraints left unstated against 7 of 8 with them stated - suggestive, not significant, and I'm not rounding that up.
  • I documented nine classes of silent instrument failure, and a tenth in the repair that closed them - each one would have printed a plausible wrong number under a passing test suite.
Why this shaped Re-Forge. The promotion ladder here - note, rule, hook - is the same shape I use in Re-Forge's candidate ladder: observed, ready for review, approved. And the lesson about silent instrument failure is why Re-Forge treats every benchmark number as something to be re-derived from the trace, not trusted because a dashboard displayed it.

The line I keep coming back to from this one: the instrument, not the search, is the result.
