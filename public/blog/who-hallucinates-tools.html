Who Hallucinates Tools, How Often, and What Fixes It?

May 2026 7 min read
Research LLM Agents Tool Use
Read on OpenReview →

This one was led by S Akash, with me as co-author, also accepted as a poster to the SCALE Workshop at ICML 2026. It's about a specific, measurable failure mode in tool-using agents: a model calling a tool that was never in its tool list — tool-name hallucination — and what actually makes it stop.

The problem

Tool-name hallucination is a known failure mode for agents that call functions, but most discussion of it is qualitative. We wanted a number: define a per-call Tool Fabrication Rate (TFR) and measure it directly on the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) multi-turn benchmark, across enough calls and enough model families that the number means something.

The headline result. Across 6,000+ opaque-baseline calls, every commercial frontier model we tested (five Anthropic 4.x versions, and OpenAI's gpt-4.x/4o/gpt-5 tiers) logged zero tool-name fabrications. Open-weight models — Qwen3, Qwen2.5, Llama — fabricated tools at up to 10.4%.

Why it matters more than it sounds

A fabricated tool name isn't always a harmless dead end. Of the fabricated calls in the open-weight models, 70% named execution-ready, state-changing actions — things like a fabricated place_order or cancel_booking call. Under a permissive executor that doesn't strictly validate against a registry before acting, that isn't a failed lookup, it's an unintended action. That's the part that makes the failure rate more than an academic curiosity for anyone deploying agents against real APIs.

The fix: Registry-Visible Reprompting

The fix we propose, RVR (Registry-Visible Reprompting), is deliberately boring: it's training-free middleware. On a registry miss — the model calls a tool that doesn't exist — RVR returns exactly one re-prompt wrapping a structured tool_not_found envelope, and lets the model try again with that signal. Across the pooled Qwen3 evaluation, RVR removed all twenty fabrications we'd logged (Fisher's exact test, p = 3.5×10-5 on 20/1,417 vs. 0/945).

The ablation that surprised us

The more interesting finding is why RVR works. We ran a powered ablation swapping in a deliberately wrong registry list in place of the real one — and it recovered just as well (Fisher's exact, p = 1.0). In other words, the content of the registry doesn't drive the fix. What drives it is the format of the rejection: telling the model, in a structured way, that its call didn't resolve is enough on its own to make it stop fabricating and try a real tool. That's a cheaper and more portable fix than it first looks, since it means you don't need a perfectly maintained tool registry for the mitigation to work — you need the rejection message shaped right.

The honest takeaway. Frontier commercial models don't currently need this fix on BFCL. Open-weight models deploying agentic tool-use in production do, and the fix is cheap: no fine-tuning, no retraining, just a well-formatted re-prompt on a registry miss.

We release the evaluation harness, the traces, and the per-call labels alongside the paper, so the TFR numbers here can be checked and extended by anyone running BFCL.
